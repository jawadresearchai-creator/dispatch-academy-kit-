Hours-of-service (HOS) rules exist because tired drivers cause crashes. Every U.S. truck driver in interstate commerce works under them, and most record their hours on an electronic logging device (ELD) like the one pictured. As a dispatcher you never touch the logs, but you must plan every load inside them.

## The four clocks

*Lesson 4.1 · The rules*

Lesson 4.1 · The rules

# The four clocks

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/big4.jpg)

### The FMCSA rules, word for word (property carriers)

- **11-hour driving limit:** “May drive a maximum of 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty.”
- **14-hour limit:** “May not drive beyond the 14th consecutive hour after coming on duty, following 10 consecutive hours off duty.”
- **30-minute break:** required “when they have driven for a period of 8 cumulative hours without at least a 30-minute interruption.”
- **60/70-hour limit:** “May not drive after 60/70 hours on duty in 7/8 consecutive days.” A 34-hour off-duty period restarts it.

> **[KEY RULE] Memorise it as one sentence**
>
> **“After 10 off: 11 driving, inside 14, break after 8, and never over 70 in 8.”**

> **[FACT] Pilot programs are not new rules**
>
> In August 2026 FMCSA announced two pilot programs (a flexible sleeper split and a way to pause the 14-hour window for up to 3 hours). Pre-tests ran in 2026 with a handful of drivers; a full test with 512 drivers is planned for 2027. **The rules on this page still apply to everyone else.**

## 11 and 14: the two daily limits

*Lesson 4.1 · The rules*

Lesson 4.1 · The rules

# 11 and 14: the two daily limits

#### The 11-hour driving limit

Counts only **driving** time. After 10 consecutive hours off, the driver has 11 hours of driving. When they are used up, he can still do other work (fuel, paperwork) but **cannot drive** until he takes another 10 hours off.

#### The 14-hour window

Starts when the driver first comes **on duty** (any work, not just driving) and runs for 14 hours straight. **It does not stop** for breaks, meals, waiting or loading. After the 14th hour, no more driving until 10 hours off.

## Three worked examples

| The driver’s day | 11-hour clock | 14-hour clock | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On duty 06:00, drives 11 h with a 30-min break and 1 h of other work, off at 18:30 | 11 h used — limit reached | Window closes 20:00 | **Legal.** Stopped by the 11-hour limit. |
| On duty 06:00, waits 5 h at the shipper, drives from 13:30 | Can drive until 20:00 = 6.5 h | Window closes 20:00 | **Stopped by the 14-hour window** with 2.5 h of driving unused. |
| On duty 05:00, drives 4 h, 1 h breakfast off duty, drives 7 h | 11 h at 17:00 (break taken) | Window closes 19:00 | **Legal.** The breakfast hour counted toward the window, not the 11. |

> **[KEY RULE] The question that matters most**
>
> When a driver says “I have 8 hours,” ask: **“8 hours of driving, or 8 hours left in your shift?”** They are different clocks, and the smaller one decides.

## The 30-minute break

*Lesson 4.1 · The rules*

Lesson 4.1 · The rules

# The 30-minute break

### What triggers it

**8 cumulative hours of driving** without an interruption of at least 30 consecutive minutes. Cumulative means driving time adds up across stops shorter than 30 minutes: 3 h + 15-min fuel stop + 5 h = 8 h, and a break is now due.

### What counts as the break

Any **30 consecutive minutes** not driving: off duty, sleeper berth, **or on duty not driving** (e.g. loading), or a mix taken back to back. So a 45-minute loading stop after 6 hours of driving resets the 8-hour count.

| Sequence | Break needed? |
| --- | --- |
| Drive 5 h → load 1 h → drive 5 h | **No** — loading 1 h interrupted the driving |
| Drive 4 h → fuel 20 min → drive 4 h | **Yes** — 20 min is too short; 8 h reached |
| Drive 8 h straight | **Yes** — 30 min before more driving |
| Drive 7 h → sleeper 10 h → drive 7 h | **No** — the rest period reset everything |

> **[FACT] Planning shortcut**
>
> For plans, add **30 minutes for every 8 hours of driving**, unless a stop at a shipper or receiver already gives the driver 30 minutes.

## Where breaks fit in a real day

| Situation | How the break is handled | Dispatcher tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Long run, no stops** | Driver stops at a truck stop or rest area after ≤ 8 h of driving | Add 30 min to the ETA |
| **Pickup mid-day** | 30+ min of loading counts as the break | No extra time needed |
| **Fuel + meal stop** | If 30 consecutive minutes, it is the break | Suggest combining them |
| **Parking at night** | Truck parking is scarce in many areas after dark | Plan the rest stop before the window closes |

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Truck parking is a real constraint**
>
> A driver whose window closes at 20:00 must be **parked** by then, not just “near” a truck stop. On the East Coast and near big cities, truck stops can be full by early evening. Ask the driver where he plans to park.

## The weekly limit: 60/7 or 70/8

*Lesson 4.1 · The rules*

Lesson 4.1 · The rules

# The weekly limit: 60/7 or 70/8

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/cycle.jpg)

### Which one applies?

A carrier that does **not** operate every day of the week uses 60 hours in 7 days. A carrier that operates **every day** may use 70 hours in 8 days. Most long-haul carriers use **70/8**. The ELD knows which one the driver is on.

**All on-duty time counts** toward the cycle, driving and non-driving (loading, waiting, inspections).

> **[KEY RULE] Why dispatchers watch the cycle**
>
> A driver with only 4 cycle hours left can’t take a 10-hour trip today, however fresh he feels. On a Friday you may need to plan a **34-hour restart** at home, or give hours back by planning short days.

> **[TRY IT] Try it**
>
> A driver has worked 11, 12, 10, 13, 0, 9, 11 hours in the last 7 days (70/8). How many hours can he work today? (Answer at the back.)

## The sleeper-berth split

*Lesson 4.1 · The rules*

Lesson 4.1 · The rules

# The sleeper-berth split

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/sleeper.jpg)

### The rule in plain words

A driver with a sleeper berth can split the 10-hour rest into two periods: one of **at least 7 consecutive hours in the sleeper berth**, and another of **at least 2 hours** (off duty or sleeper). Together they must add up to **at least 10 hours**. When paired correctly, neither period counts against the 14-hour window.

FMCSA’s summary lists the pairings: 7/3, 8/2, 7.5/2.5 and so on.

> **[KEY RULE] How dispatchers use it**
>
> A driver stuck for 3 hours at a slow shipper might log that time in the sleeper and use it as the short half of a split. That can save a delivery. But the **calculation belongs to the driver and his ELD**. You ask; you don’t decide.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Beginner mistake**
>
> Planning a trip that only works “if he splits” without asking the driver. Many drivers dislike splits because the sleep is broken. Plan on a normal 10-hour rest unless the driver offers a split.

## Adverse conditions, short-haul, PC and yard moves

*Lesson 4.1 · Special rules*

Lesson 4.1 · Special rules

# Adverse conditions, short-haul, PC and yard moves

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/exceptions.jpg)

| Rule | Plain meaning | Dispatcher note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Adverse driving conditions** | 11-h and 14-h limits can be extended by up to 2 hours for conditions the driver didn’t know about when he started (snow, fog, a closure) | Never plan a trip that depends on it |
| **Short-haul exception** | Within 150 air miles, back within 14 hours: time records instead of logs | Common for local box trucks. Ask the carrier which drivers use it |
| **Personal conveyance** | Off-duty personal movement of the truck | Never to advance a load; FMCSA guidance allows e.g. moving to the nearest safe rest spot |
| **Yard move** | Movement inside a yard, logged as on duty | Not for public roads |

## The rules on one page

*Lesson 4.1 · Summary*

Lesson 4.1 · Summary

# The rules on one page

| Rule | Limit | Resets with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Driving** | 11 h | 10 consecutive hours off (or valid sleeper split) |
| **Window** | No driving after the 14th hour on duty | 10 consecutive hours off (or valid split) |
| **Break** | 30 min after 8 cumulative hours of driving | Any 30 consecutive minutes not driving |
| **Cycle** | 60 h / 7 days or 70 h / 8 days on duty | Rolling; or a 34-hour off-duty restart |
| **Sleeper split** | ≥ 7 h sleeper + ≥ 2 h, total ≥ 10 | — |
| **Adverse conditions** | + up to 2 h on the 11 and 14 | Unexpected conditions only |
| **Short-haul** | 150 air miles, 14-hour day | Time records instead of logs |

## Miles per day, realistically

≈ 500–550

**miles on a full solo day**: 11 h × about 50 mph average including towns, traffic and fuel.

≈ 350–450

**miles on a day with a pickup or delivery**, because stops eat into the 14-hour window.

≈ 1,000+

**miles per 24 h for a team**, two drivers taking turns, each within his own limits.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Why “600 miles a day” is dangerous**
>
> If you promise a broker 600+ miles a day for a solo driver, the trip only works if he speeds, skips breaks or falsifies his logs. Plan on 500–550 on full driving days and less on pickup and delivery days.

## What an ELD is and what it records

*Lesson 4.2 · The ELD*

Lesson 4.2 · The ELD

# What an ELD is and what it records

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/eld.jpg)

### How it works

The ELD connects to the truck’s engine. When the truck moves, it switches the driver to **Driving** automatically and records time, location and engine data. The driver selects the other statuses. Officers at roadside inspections can view or receive the logs.

Carriers usually give dispatchers a login to the ELD provider’s **web portal**, where you can see every driver’s clocks and location.

> **[FACT] If the ELD breaks (49 CFR 395.34)**
>
> The driver notes the malfunction, notifies the carrier in writing within 24 hours, rebuilds his logs on paper for today and the previous 7 days, and keeps paper logs until the ELD is fixed. The carrier has 8 days to repair or replace it.

> **[KEY RULE] You read the ELD; you never edit it**
>
> Drivers can propose edits for genuine mistakes (e.g. forgot to switch to off duty), and the ELD keeps the original record. **Asking a driver to change his hours so a load “works” is illegal.**

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/log_legal.jpg)

### How to read it

- Four rows: **off duty, sleeper berth, driving, on duty not driving**.
- The line moves between rows as the driver’s status changes; the grid covers 24 hours, midnight to midnight.

# Four numbers before every booking

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/panel.jpg)

### How to ask when you have no portal access

> **[SAY IT OUT LOUD] Say it out loud — asking for hours**
>
> **“Hi Carlos, before I book tomorrow’s load: how many drive hours and shift hours do you have left right now, and how much on your 70? When does your break come due?”**

> **[KEY RULE] Write them down with the time**
>
> “Carlos, Tue 21:10 PKT: drive 6:12 · shift 8:40 · break in 2:10 · cycle 31:15.” The clocks change every minute; the note shows when you asked.

### What the four numbers let you do

| Number | Question it answers | Example use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drive left** | How far can he drive today? | 6:12 × 50 ≈ 300 miles more today |
| **Shift left** | When must he stop, even with drive hours left? | Can he still load (1.5 h) and then drive? |
| **Break in** | When is a 30-min stop due? | Add 30 min to the ETA if no loading stop comes first |
| **Cycle left** | Can he do a multi-day trip this week? | A 3-day trip needs ≈ 30+ on-duty hours |

## The feasibility formula

*Lesson 4.3 · Feasibility*

Lesson 4.3 · Feasibility

# The feasibility formula

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/formula.jpg)

### The method in one line

**Start time + stop time + driving (miles ÷ 50) + breaks + rest periods + buffer = ETA.** Convert the ETA to the **receiver’s time zone** and compare with the appointment. Then check that the driver’s current clocks and cycle allow it.

> **[FACT] Why 50 mph?**
>
> Trucks often cruise at 60–70 mph on interstates, but fuel stops, towns, traffic, weigh stations and construction bring the real average down. 50 mph is a common, safe planning figure. Some carriers use 45 (cities, mountains) or 55 (open highway).

## A three-day trip: Laredo → Chicago

*Lesson 4.3 · Trip plan 3*

Lesson 4.3 · Trip plan 3

# A three-day trip: Laredo → Chicago

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/trip_lar_chi.jpg)

### How to estimate long trips fast

1. Divide the miles into full driving days of about **550 miles** (11 h × 50).
2. Reduce the first day for loading (and the last for unloading).
3. Add a **10-hour rest** between days.
4. 1,430 mi → 550 + 550 + 330 = **3 driving days**.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] When a broker asks for the impossible**
>
> “Pick up Laredo Monday, deliver Chicago Tuesday” is ~1,430 miles in about 30 hours. That is **team** freight. Say so politely: *“That’s a team transit. With a solo driver the earliest is Wednesday midday.”*

> **[FACT] Team drivers**
>
> Two drivers take turns: one drives up to 11 h while the other rests in the sleeper. A team can cover 1,000+ miles in 24 hours. Only offer team service if the carrier really has a team.

## Yes, no, or “only if…”

*Lesson 4.3 · The decision*

Lesson 4.3 · The decision

# Yes, no, or “only if…”

| Your calculation shows… | Decision | What you say / do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ETA well before the appointment** (2 h+ buffer), clocks and cycle OK | YES | Proceed to negotiate and book. |
| **ETA within 1 h of the appointment** | ONLY IF | Ask for a later appointment or FCFS window; tell the carrier the risk. |
| **ETA after the appointment** | NO (as posted) | “Can the delivery move to…?” If not, don’t book. |
| **Driver can’t reach pickup on time** with his current hours | NO | Don’t promise it. Offer the realistic pickup time. |
| **Not enough cycle hours** | NO or re-plan | Plan a restart or a shorter load. |
| **Only works with a split, team or adverse-conditions extension** | ONLY IF | Only if the driver or carrier confirms it is real and safe. |

> **[KEY RULE] The honest-ETA habit**
>
> Brokers remember dispatchers whose trucks arrive when promised. It is better to lose a load by being honest than to book a load that arrives late, costs a fee, and damages the carrier’s name.

## Common hour-eaters to ask about

#### Live load / unload

Driver waits while freight is loaded: 1–3 h, sometimes more.

#### Detention history

“How long do they usually take?” Brokers often know.

#### Big cities

Traffic in LA, Chicago, NYC, Atlanta can double the time.

#### Weather & mountains

Snow, wind, grades: slower average speed.

## Before you say “yes” to a load

*Lesson 4.3 · Checklist*

Lesson 4.3 · Checklist

# Before you say “yes” to a load

### The driver

- Where is he now, and when will he be empty?
- Drive hours, shift hours, break due, cycle hours **right now**
- When does his 10-hour rest end?
- Home-time plans, personal limits (e.g. no night driving)

### The load

- Pickup window: appointment or FCFS? Live load or drop?
- Miles from the truck to pickup (deadhead) + loaded miles
- Delivery window and **time zone**
- Known delays: detention history, city, weather, mountains

### The calculation

- Drive time = miles ÷ 50
- + 30-min breaks · + 10-h rests · + stop time · + buffer
- ETA converted to the receiver’s zone
- Buffer of at least 1–2 hours?

> **[SAY IT OUT LOUD] Say it out loud — the honest ETA**
>
> **“With a solo driver, loading at 9 Central, I can have him in Charlotte around 2 PM Eastern Thursday, but that’s tight. Could the receiver take him at 4, or first come first served?”**

> **[KEY RULE] Deadhead counts too**
>
> The drive from where the truck is now to the pickup uses the same clocks. A load 150 miles away costs 3 hours of driving before loading even starts.

## What a dispatcher must never do

*Safety*

Safety

# What a dispatcher must never do

![](/assets/diagrams/m04/coercion.jpg)

### The coercion rule

49 CFR 390.6: a motor carrier, shipper, receiver or transportation intermediary (and their agents) may not coerce a driver to operate a truck in violation of the safety rules, including hours of service. FMCSA’s rule came into force in 2016, and drivers can report coercion to FMCSA.

As the carrier’s dispatcher you are acting for the carrier. A message like “Just run it, nobody will know” is exactly the evidence an investigator looks for.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Falsified logs**
>
> Editing, faking or “fixing” records of duty status to hide violations is illegal for the driver and the carrier. ELDs keep an audit trail of every edit. If a trip only works with a false log, **it doesn’t work**.

> **[KEY RULE] Your script when the driver says no**
>
> **“Understood — safety first. I’ll let the broker know and re-plan.”** Then do exactly that, early.

## Glossary

*Reference*

Reference

# Glossary

**11-hour limit** — Maximum driving after 10 consecutive hours off.

**14-hour window** — Period after coming on duty; no driving after its end.

**30-minute break** — Required after 8 cumulative hours of driving.

**34-hour restart** — 34+ consecutive hours off duty that resets the 60/70 cycle.

**60/70-hour cycle** — On-duty limit over 7 or 8 consecutive days.

**Adverse driving conditions** — Unexpected conditions allowing up to 2 extra hours.

**Coercion** — Forcing a driver to break safety rules; banned by 49 CFR 390.6.

**Cycle hours** — On-duty hours left in the 7/8-day period.

**Deadhead** — Empty miles to reach a pickup; uses the same clocks.

**Drive time left** — ELD figure: driving hours remaining today.

**ELD** — Electronic logging device recording duty status.

**FCFS** — First come, first served: a window instead of a fixed time.

**HOS** — Hours of service: federal driver work-time rules.

**Home terminal time** — Time standard used on the driver’s log.

**Live load / unload** — Driver waits while freight is loaded or unloaded.

**Off duty** — Not working.

**On duty (not driving)** — Working but not driving: loading, inspections, waiting.

**Personal conveyance** — Off-duty personal use of the truck; never to advance a load.

**RODS** — Record of duty status (the log).

**Short-haul exception** — 150 air miles / 14 h: time records instead of logs.

**Shift left** — Time remaining in the 14-hour window.

**Sleeper berth** — Rest in the truck’s sleeping compartment.

**Split sleeper** — 7+ h sleeper + 2+ h rest = the 10-hour rest.

**Team** — Two drivers taking turns so the truck keeps moving.

**Yard move** — On-duty movement inside a yard.
