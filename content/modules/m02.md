# How to use this module

In Module 1 you learned who is who. Now you learn **what** moves the freight. Every load you will ever consider starts with one check: **does this freight physically and operationally fit this truck?**

### Study rhythm (about 7 hours)

- **Session 1 (1.5 h):** the basics — anatomy, weight, size. Read slowly; everything else builds on it.
- **Session 2 (2 h):** dry van and box truck. Learn the spec sheets until you can draw them.
- **Session 3 (2 h):** reefer, flatbed, step deck, power only, Sprinter.
- **Session 4 (1.5 h):** matching, codes, mistakes, the practice postings, the visual quiz and the check.

### How to read a spec sheet

Each equipment type has a one-page spec sheet: a **real photo**, a **side drawing with dimensions**, a **spec table**, the **questions to ask before booking**, and its current **DAT equipment types**. Numbers are **typical**. Trucks differ, so the dispatcher’s habit is always: *confirm with the carrier.*

>
> Your equipment PDFs are visually excellent but contain several outdated or over-simplified claims (fixed payloads, “13′6″ federal height”, “4 chains per load”, “pre-cool 2 hours”, old Sprinter codes, CDL by box length). Each correction is flagged with a red box in this module.

## Eight kinds of equipment you will dispatch

*Lesson 2.0 · The equipment family*

Lesson 2.0 · The equipment family

# Eight kinds of equipment you will dispatch

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/lineup.jpg)

### Three big families

- **Enclosed:** dry van, reefer, box truck, Sprinter van, curtainside. Freight is protected from weather; loaded from the rear (or side, for curtainside).
- **Open deck:** flatbed, step deck and specialised trailers. Freight that is too big, heavy or awkward for a box; loaded by forklift or crane; must be secured and often tarped.
- **Capacity only:** power only — a tractor that pulls someone else’s trailer.

> **[KEY RULE] The first check on every load**
>
> Before rate, before broker, before anything: **can this truck carry this freight — size, weight, temperature, loading method and gear?** If the answer is “no” or “not sure,” you are not ready to call.

Which equipment will you dispatch most? In Pakistani dispatch offices, beginners usually start with **dry van, box truck and power only**. Reefer and flatbed often come later because they add temperature and securement risk.

## Tractor-trailer vs straight truck

*Lesson 2.0 · Anatomy*

Lesson 2.0 · Anatomy

# Tractor-trailer vs straight truck

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/anatomy.jpg)

### Words you must use correctly

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Tractor** (power unit) | The truck with the engine and cab. Pulls a trailer. |
| **Semi-trailer** | The trailer. Its front rests on the tractor’s fifth wheel. |
| **Fifth wheel / kingpin** | The coupling. The trailer’s kingpin locks into the tractor’s fifth wheel. |
| **Landing gear** | Legs that hold the trailer up when it is dropped. |
| **Tandem axles** | Two axles together. Trailer tandems often slide to balance weight. |
| **Straight truck** | Cab and cargo box on one frame, e.g. a box truck. |

### Why the difference matters to you

A tractor can **drop** one trailer and **hook** another. That makes **drop-and-hook** loads and **power-only** work possible. A straight truck cannot drop its box: it must wait while it is loaded and unloaded.

Weight and licensing also differ. Tractor-trailers are combination vehicles (Class A CDL). Box trucks are single vehicles whose licence depends on their weight rating (page 14).

> **[FACT] Three weight words**
>
> **GVWR** = the maximum a vehicle is rated to weigh, loaded (on a door sticker). **Tare** = what it weighs empty. **Payload** = what it can carry = the legal maximum minus the tare.

## 80,000 lb is the whole truck, not the cargo

*Lesson 2.0 · The weight truth*

Lesson 2.0 · The weight truth

# 80,000 lb is the whole truck, not the cargo

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/weight.jpg)

### The federal numbers

On the Interstate system, FHWA’s rules are: **80,000 lb gross** (unless the bridge formula gives less), **20,000 lb on a single axle**, **34,000 lb on a tandem axle**. The **bridge formula** limits weight on groups of axles according to how far apart they are, to protect bridges.

So the cargo is whatever remains after the **tractor, fuel, driver and trailer** are counted, and only if it is spread so that no axle group is overweight.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “Dry van max payload = 45,000 lb” / “Flatbed max = 48,000 lb”**
>
> These are rough real-world examples, **not legal limits**. A heavy tractor and a steel trailer carry less; a light tractor and an aluminium trailer carry more. Your old manual also claimed “mountain areas lower payload to 30–40k” — there is no such general rule.

> **[KEY RULE] The dispatcher habit**
>
> Every truck gets a **“max weight”** in your notes, confirmed by the carrier: *“Truck 12 legal up to 44,500.”* Never book to a generic number from a PDF.

## Width, length and height: who sets the limit?

*Lesson 2.0 · Size rules*

Lesson 2.0 · Size rules

# Width, length and height: who sets the limit?

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/sizes.jpg)

### What FHWA says

- **Width:** 102 inches (8′6″) is the federal maximum on the National Network. Wider = over-dimension.
- **Length:** states must allow semitrailers of **at least 48′** on the National Network; many allow 53′ (and some longer, grandfathered).
- **Height:** “There is no Federal vehicle height requirement.” States set their own — most between **13′6″ and 14′**.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “13′6″ is the legal height”**
>
> There is **no single federal height limit**. 13′6″ is common in the East; 14′ is common in many Western states; bridges and tunnels can be lower. Flatbed and step-deck freight must be checked against the route.

> **[FACT] What this means for vans**
>
> Vans are built to about 13′6″ tall so they can run almost everywhere. Height becomes your problem mainly with **open-deck** freight (pages 22–24).

## The 53-foot dry van

*Lesson 2.1 · Dry van*

Lesson 2.1 · Dry van

# The 53-foot dry van

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_dv53.jpg)

### Read the sheet like a dispatcher

The two numbers that decide most van loads are **pallet positions (26)** and **legal weight**. A load of 26 light pallets fills the floor long before it reaches the weight limit: it **cubes out**. A load of 18 pallets of paper can reach the weight limit with floor space left: it **weighs out**.

Vans load at **docks**. The trailer floor sits about 4 feet off the ground, the same height as a warehouse dock, so forklifts drive straight in.

### What dry vans haul

Palletised or boxed goods that need weather protection but **no temperature control**: retail and grocery dry goods, paper, packaging, beverages, electronics, household goods, auto parts — like the printed boxes on load #12354.

### What they can’t

Frozen or temperature-sensitive food (reefer), freight taller than about 9′ or wider than the doors, and freight that must be loaded from the top or side with a crane (flatbed / curtainside).

## Pallet math: cube vs weight

*Lesson 2.1 · Dry van*

Lesson 2.1 · Dry van

# Pallet math: cube vs weight

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/pallets.jpg)

### Straight vs turned

A standard U.S. pallet is **48″ × 40″**. Loaded **straight** (48″ side along the trailer), two pallets fit side by side and 13 rows fit in a 53′ van: **26 pallets**. **Turned** (40″ along the trailer, 48″ + 48″ across), 15 rows fit: **up to 30**, but only if the trailer is wide enough inside and the shipper loads it that way.

> **[TRY IT] Worked example**
>
> A posting says: **“24 pallets, 42,000 lb, 53′ dry van.”** Floor: 24 ≤ 26 ✓. Weight: 42,000 lb — you must ask the carrier whether this truck is legal at 42,000. If the truck’s max is 43,500 ✓. If it is 41,000 ✗.

### Questions that uncover problems

- **“Palletised or floor-loaded?”** Floor-loaded (loose boxes) takes hours to load and unload by hand.
- **“Stackable?”** Some pallets can be double-stacked, which changes the space needed.
- **“Pallet size?”** Not every pallet is 48 × 40; oversized pallets mean fewer fit.
- **“Exact weight?”** Remember load #12354: the RC said 2,000 lb, the BOL said 5,729 lb.

> **[KEY RULE] Say it back**
>
> “So that’s **24 pallets, non-stackable, 42,000 pounds**, dock to dock.” Reading back catches mistakes on the call itself.

## The 48-foot dry van — and when length matters

*Lesson 2.1 · Dry van*

Lesson 2.1 · Dry van

# The 48-foot dry van — and when length matters

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_dv48.jpg)

### Why 48′ still exists

Older docks, tight city streets, some yards and some customers were built around shorter trailers. A few postings say **“48′ max”** or **“no 53′”**. Most carriers today run 53′ vans; some also have 48′.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Classic mistake: wrong length**
>
> The receiver only accepts 48′ trailers. Your 53′ shows up and is turned away at the gate: no delivery, redelivery fees, a furious broker. **Ask:** “Any length restriction at pickup or delivery?”

| Question | 48′ van | 53′ van |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pallets (straight / turned)** | 24 / up to 28 | 26 / up to 30 |
| **Weight capacity** | Similar — depends on the truck | Similar — depends on the truck |
| **Fits “48′ max” locations** | ✓ | ✗ |

> **[TRY IT] Quick check**
>
> A load is **25 pallets, 20,000 lb, “48′ max at delivery.”** Can your 48′ van take it? No: 25 pallets needs more than 24 straight positions, unless the shipper can turn them. Ask the broker how the pallets are loaded.

## Inside the van: gear, doors and variants

*Lesson 2.1 · Dry van*

Lesson 2.1 · Dry van

# Inside the van: gear, doors and variants

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/vangear.jpg)

### Securement applies to vans too

FMCSA’s cargo-securement rules apply to **all cargo-carrying commercial vehicles** in interstate commerce, including enclosed vans. Freight that shifts can break through doors, tip the trailer or be damaged. Loads may require **straps, load bars or blocking**, and brokers sometimes ask for “2 load bars” or “straps required.”

### Swing doors vs roll-up door

Most 53′ vans have **swing doors**. Some vans and many box trucks have a **roll-up door**, which sits inside the opening and can reduce usable door height. It rarely matters, until the freight is tall.

#### Curtainside van (DAT: VC)

Side curtains slide open so a forklift can load from the side. Used for rolls, long items, or where rear loading is impossible.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “Curtain-side is still filtered under V”**
>
> DAT now lists **Van w/ Curtains (VC)** as its own type. Use current platform categories, not old cheat sheets.

## Van variants and what they really mean

*Lesson 2.1 · Dry van*

Lesson 2.1 · Dry van

# Van variants and what they really mean

| DAT type | Name | What it means | When it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **V** | Van | Standard enclosed dry van | The default for general freight |
| **VA** | Van Air-Ride | Air-bag suspension gives a softer ride | When the posting / broker requires air-ride |
| **VM** | Van w/ Team | Two drivers take turns so the truck keeps moving | Time-critical loads that need team service |
| **VC** | Van w/ Curtains | Curtainside, side-loading | Side loading required |
| **VG** | Van Lift Gate | Van with a liftgate | No-dock delivery with a van |
| **VZ** | Van Hazmat | Van hauling placarded hazardous materials | Only if carrier + driver are hazmat-qualified |
| **VP** | Van w/ Pallet Exchange | Driver swaps empty pallets for loaded ones | Pallet exchange loads — ask how it works |
| **VV** | Van Vented | Van with vents for airflow | Some produce / commodities needing ventilation |

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “VA is mandatory for fragile freight”**
>
> Air-ride is a **load requirement when the posting, broker or shipper asks for it**, not an automatic rule for glass or electronics. Read the requirements and ask.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “VM = loads over 700 miles”**
>
> VM means a **van with a team**. No 700-mile rule exists. Use it when the load needs team service **and** your carrier has team drivers.

> **[FACT] “VR — van or reefer”?**
>
> Older manuals teach VR. It did not appear in DAT’s current equipment list when this module was checked (Sep 2026), so it is not taught as a core code. Check the live platform in Module 6.

## The straight box truck

*Lesson 2.2 · Box truck*

Lesson 2.2 · Box truck

# The straight box truck

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_box26.jpg)

### Where box trucks shine

Shorter local and regional runs, partial loads, deliveries to **stores, homes, job sites and businesses without docks**, and freight too small for a 53′ van. Many new small carriers and owner-operators start with a box truck, so you may dispatch them early in your career.

### The numbers that trip beginners

- **Payload is small** — often 8,000–12,000 lb, not 45,000.
- **Interior sizes vary** — ask for inside length, width, height and door opening.
- **Liftgate is not standard** — confirm it exists and its capacity.

Remember load #12354: a 26′ box truck, and the BOL weight was nearly three times the RC weight. On a box truck that difference really matters.

## Who can drive it? CDL and the 26,001 lb line

*Lesson 2.2 · Box truck*

Lesson 2.2 · Box truck

# Who can drive it? CDL and the 26,001 lb line

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/cdl.jpg)

### FMCSA’s classes, in plain words

- **Class A:** combinations of 26,001 lb or more where the towed unit is over 10,000 lb — every tractor-trailer.
- **Class B:** a **single vehicle** with a GVWR of 26,001 lb or more — heavier box trucks.
- **Class C:** smaller vehicles that carry **placarded hazmat** or **16+ passengers**.

A box truck rated at **25,999 lb** and driven without hazmat usually needs **no CDL**. The same-size box rated at **33,000 lb** needs a **Class B**.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “14–22 ft = non-CDL, 24–28 ft = CDL”**
>
> The licence depends on the **GVWR and the use** (hazmat, passengers), **not the length of the box**. Two 26′ trucks can need different licences.

> **[KEY RULE] Where to find the GVWR**
>
> The manufacturer’s certification label on the **driver’s door jamb**. Ask the carrier for it when you onboard a box truck and write it in your truck notes.

## Dock or no dock? Liftgate and pallet jack

*Lesson 2.2 · Box truck*

Lesson 2.2 · Box truck

# Dock or no dock? Liftgate and pallet jack

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/dock.jpg)

### The questions for every box-truck stop

- “Is there a **dock** at pickup and at delivery?”
- “Is it **residential** or a business with a dock?”
- “Is a **liftgate** required? Does the driver need a **pallet jack**?”
- “Is there a **forklift** on site, or does the driver unload?”
- “How heavy is the heaviest pallet?” (It must be within liftgate capacity.)

#### Reefer box truck (DAT: Straight Box Truck – Reefer)

Box trucks can also be refrigerated. Treat them like a reefer: setpoint, mode, pre-cool if required.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “VG is the box-truck liftgate code”**
>
> DAT lists VG as **Van Lift Gate**. Box trucks have their own types: **Straight Box Truck**, **Straight Box Truck – Reefer**, **Straight Box Truck Hazmat**. Treat the liftgate as a **capability you confirm**, not a code to memorise.

## The refrigerated trailer (reefer)

*Lesson 2.3 · Reefer*

Lesson 2.3 · Reefer

# The refrigerated trailer (reefer)

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_reefer.jpg)

### Why a reefer holds a bit less

Thick insulated walls, floor and roof make the inside **narrower and lower** than a dry van, and the unit, insulation and fuel make the trailer **heavier**, so it can carry less weight. Its floor has **grooves** so cold air can return to the unit underneath the pallets.

**The unit** on the front wall: a diesel engine and compressor with its own fuel tank.

### Continuous vs cycle (start-stop)

**Continuous:** the unit runs all the time and air always moves, the usual choice for fresh produce and many sensitive loads. **Cycle / start-stop:** the unit switches off when the temperature is reached and restarts when needed. It saves fuel and is often used for frozen goods. **The shipper decides**; the RC or BOL usually says which.

**Inside:** the air chute along the ceiling, insulated walls, grooved floor.

## Cold air, and what “temperature control” means

*Lesson 2.3 · Reefer*

Lesson 2.3 · Reefer

# Cold air, and what “temperature control” means

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/reefer_air.jpg)

### Three kinds of reefer freight (examples)

| Type | Typical example | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Frozen** | Ice cream, frozen meat — often around 0°F or below | Setpoint, cycle vs continuous, pre-cool |
| **Chilled / fresh** | Dairy, fresh meat — often mid-30s °F | Tight temperature windows |
| **Produce** | Fruit, vegetables — varies by product | Usually continuous; some products must not freeze |

These are examples to build intuition. **The only temperature that counts is the one on the load instructions / RC.**

> **[KEY RULE] A reefer can often haul dry freight**
>
> With the unit off, a reefer is an insulated van. Carriers often take dry loads as backhauls. Ask the broker whether a reefer is acceptable for a van load, and check the extra trailer weight.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “No temperature log = no claim defence”**
>
> Too absolute. Temperature records are **important evidence**, and FDA’s rule says carriers must be able to show they kept the requested temperature **when asked**. Outcomes of claims depend on the contract and the facts.

## Before you book a reefer: five questions

*Lesson 2.3 · Reefer*

Lesson 2.3 · Reefer

# Before you book a reefer: five questions

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/reefer_flow.jpg)

1. **“What is the setpoint?”** Write the exact number and °F. Never guess.
2. **“Continuous or cycle?”**
3. **“Is pre-cooling required, and to what?”**
4. **“Frozen, chilled or produce? What temperature is the product loaded at?”**
5. **“Any temperature recording or download needed at delivery?”**

> **[SAY IT OUT LOUD] Say it out loud — reefer read-back**
>
> “So that’s **34 degrees Fahrenheit, continuous**, pre-cool to 34 before loading, fresh chicken, temperature download at delivery. Correct?”

### What FDA’s rule actually says

The FSMA sanitary-transportation rule says that before loading food that needs temperature control, the loader must make sure the refrigerated compartment is **“adequately prepared … including precooling, if necessary.”** Shipper and carrier can agree how temperature is monitored, and carriers must show they kept the requested temperature **“only upon request.”**

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “Pre-cool the trailer 2 hours before loading”**
>
> No universal 2-hour rule exists. **Pre-cool when the shipper or the load instructions require it**, to the temperature they give, and always verify the setpoint before loading.

Why it matters: a wrong setpoint can mean a **rejected load** worth tens of thousands of dollars and a claim against your carrier.

## The flatbed

*Lesson 2.4 · Flatbed*

Lesson 2.4 · Flatbed

# The flatbed

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_flat48.jpg)

### Why flatbed is a different world

With a van, the box protects the freight. With a flatbed, **the driver builds the protection**: straps, chains, edge protectors, blocking and tarps. Loading is usually from the **sides or top** by forklift or crane, so the freight can be long, heavy or awkward.

Flatbed loads therefore depend on **dimensions, weight and gear**, not just the letter “F.”

### Tarps in one minute

- **Lumber tarps** — large, cover tall stacks.
- **Steel tarps** — smaller, for lower loads like coils or beams.
- **“Drop”** — how far the tarp hangs down the sides: 4′, 6′, 8′. A posting may say “8′ drops required.”
- **Smoke tarp** — covers the front of a load against exhaust.

DAT now shows handling requirements (tarps, chains, coil racks) on flatbed loads. Match them to what the truck carries.

## The tiedown rule — and the “4 chains” myth

*Lesson 2.4 · Securement*

Lesson 2.4 · Securement

# The tiedown rule — and the “4 chains” myth

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/tiedowns.jpg)

### FMCSA’s general rule, in plain words

For an article that is **not blocked** from moving forward (by a headboard or other cargo): **1 tiedown** if it is 5 ft or shorter and 1,100 lb or lighter; **2 tiedowns** if longer than 5 ft (up to 10 ft) or heavier than 1,100 lb; above 10 ft, **2 for the first 10 ft plus 1 for every extra 10 ft or part of it**. The combined working load limit of all tiedowns must be **at least half the cargo’s weight**.

> **[TRY IT] Worked example**
>
> A 32-ft bundle of steel beams, not blocked: first 10 ft → 2, remaining 22 ft → 3 more (10 + 10 + 2). Minimum **5 tiedowns**, and commodity rules may require more.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “Minimum 4 chains per flatbed load”**
>
> No such universal rule exists. The count depends on **length, weight, whether the cargo is blocked, the working load limits**, and **commodity-specific rules** for logs, lumber, metal coils, paper rolls, concrete pipe, vehicles, heavy machinery and boulders.

You don’t need to calculate securement for the driver. You need to know enough to ask: “What does this load need, and does our truck have it?”

## The step deck (drop deck)

*Lesson 2.4 · Step deck*

Lesson 2.4 · Step deck

# The step deck (drop deck)

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_step53.jpg)

### How to spot one

Look for the **step**: a short upper deck over the tractor, then a drop to a long, lower deck. In the photo the lower deck is loaded with long steel pieces. A **flatbed** is one flat deck at about 5 feet.

DAT has **SD** (Step Deck) and **FD** (Flatbed or Step Deck). FD postings accept either, so height may not be critical.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Correction: “Step deck = no permit needed for medium machinery”**
>
> A step deck **reduces loaded height**, which may keep a load legal. But permit need depends on the **total dimensions and the states on the route**. It never guarantees permit-free movement.

*Lesson 2.4 · Height math*

Lesson 2.4 · Height math

# Flatbed or step deck? Do the height math

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/clearance.jpg)

> **[TRY IT] Worked example: a 9′6″ machine**
>
> On a flatbed: 5′0″ deck + 9′6″ = **14′6″**, over a 13′6″ or 14′ limit ✗. On a step deck: 3′6″ + 9′6″ = **13′0″**, under 13′6″ ✓, if every state and bridge on the route allows it. **Use real deck heights from the carrier**, not typical ones.

### Other open-deck equipment (awareness only)

| Type | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Conestoga (CN)** | Flatbed or step deck with a rolling tarp system, like a covered wagon |
| **Double drop** | Very low well between two raised decks, for tall freight |
| **RGN / lowboy** | Heavy haul; the front detaches so machinery can drive on |
| **Hotshot** | Pickup truck pulling a gooseneck flatbed, for smaller urgent loads |

#### Gooseneck trailer used in hotshot work

Real photo from your manual. Hotshot (pickup + gooseneck) is a growing niche. DAT lists Flatbed Hotshot (FH) and Van Hotshot (VH).

> **[KEY RULE] Know your limits**
>
> Heavy-haul and over-dimension freight (RGN, lowboy, permits, escorts) is a specialist job. Learn to **recognise** it and pass it on until you are trained.

## Power only: the tractor without a trailer

*Lesson 2.5 · Power only*

Lesson 2.5 · Power only

# Power only: the tractor without a trailer

![](/assets/diagrams/m02/spec_po.jpg)

### How a power-only load works

1. Your driver goes to a yard or shipper and **hooks a trailer** that is already loaded (or empty).
2. Checks the trailer: tyres, lights, brakes, doors, **seal number**. Photos before leaving.
3. Hauls it to the receiver or another yard and **drops** it.
4. Sometimes takes an **empty trailer back**. Ask whether that is paid.

> **[KEY RULE] Why beginners see many power-only loads**
>
> Some shippers and brokers keep their own trailer pools and just need tractors. Power only has **no trailer to own or maintain**, and it moves fast with drop-and-hook. DAT has also added power-only specialty variants in 2025.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] The insurance question**
>
> When your carrier pulls someone else’s trailer, the trailer owner usually wants **trailer interchange** (or similar) insurance. Confirm your carrier has it before booking.

## Hazmat, tankers, teams and over-dimension

*Lesson 2.5 · Special cases*

Lesson 2.5 · Special cases

# Hazmat, tankers, teams and over-dimension

#### Hazardous materials (hazmat)

Placarded hazmat needs a driver with a **hazmat endorsement** on the CDL and a carrier set up for hazmat (registration, insurance, procedures). DAT types include VZ, RZ, FZ and SB-Hazmat. **Never book hazmat unless your carrier and driver are qualified.**

#### Tankers

Liquids and gases in bulk. Specialised equipment, and a tanker endorsement is required for the driver. Recognise it; beginners rarely dispatch tankers.

#### Over-dimension

Wide, tall, long or heavy loads that need permits, sometimes escorts and specific routes. DAT: FO, LO.

#### Team service

Two drivers take turns driving, so the truck can move almost non-stop within hours-of-service rules. Used for urgent, long loads. DAT marks it with **VM, RM, FM** or “Team” variants. Only book team loads if your carrier really has a team on that truck.

#### Pallet exchange

The shipper or receiver swaps empty pallets for loaded ones. It can cost the carrier time or money if the pallets aren’t returned. Ask how it works (VP, RP).

> **[KEY RULE] One-line rule for special equipment**
>
> If a load has an extra letter (Z, M, P, O, T…) or the word “Hazmat / Team / Over-dimension,” **stop and confirm the carrier and driver are qualified and equipped** before you call.

## Answers — Module 2 check

*Answer key*

Answer key

# Answers — Module 2 check

**1.**

A tractor-trailer is two units: the tractor can drop one trailer and hook another. A straight truck’s box is on the same frame and can’t be dropped. Drop-and-hook and power-only work need a tractor.

**2.**

80,000 lb is the gross for the whole vehicle. Example: 80,000 − tractor/fuel/driver 19,000 − trailer 15,000 = 46,000 lb on paper, and axle limits and real loading usually make it less. Ask the carrier for the truck’s max.

**3.**

26 straight, up to 30 turned (if the inside width and loading allow); about 12 in a 26′ box truck.

**4.**

**False.** There is no federal vehicle height limit. States set limits, mostly 13′6″–14′, and routes can be lower.

**5.**

25,999 lb GVWR, no hazmat → usually **no CDL**. 33,000 lb GVWR → **Class B CDL**. Length doesn’t decide it.

**6.**

Any four: dock or no dock; residential?; liftgate required?; pallet jack?; forklift on site?; heaviest pallet weight vs liftgate capacity; inside dimensions.

**7.**

Continuous: the unit runs all the time. Cycle/start-stop: it switches off at the setpoint and restarts as needed. The shipper decides; it’s usually on the RC or BOL.

**8.**

The compartment must be adequately prepared, “including precooling, if necessary.” The old “always 2 hours” rule is not in the regulation. Follow the shipper’s instructions.

**9.**

First 10 ft → 2; remaining 15 ft → 2 more (10 + 5) = **4 tiedowns** minimum, plus any commodity-specific rules.

**10.**

Flatbed: 5′0″ + 9′10″ = **14′10″** ✗. Step deck: 3′4″ + 9′10″ = **13′2″** ✓ under 13′6″, if every state and bridge on the route allows it.

**11.**

MV = Moving Van · VH = Van Hotshot · VM = Van w/ Team · VC = Van w/ Curtains.

**12.**

The driver has a hazmat endorsement and the carrier is set up for hazmat (registration, insurance, procedures), and the truck can be placarded correctly. Otherwise, don’t book.

## Glossary

*Reference*

Reference

# Glossary

**Air-ride** — Suspension using air bags for a smoother ride (VA, RA).

**Blocking / bracing** — Wood or other material built around cargo to stop movement.

**Box truck** — Straight truck with an enclosed cargo box.

**Bridge formula** — Federal formula limiting weight on axle groups by their spacing.

**Continuous mode** — Reefer unit runs all the time.

**Cube out** — Trailer floor or space is full before reaching the weight limit.

**Curtainside** — Van with sliding side curtains for side loading (VC).

**Cycle / start-stop** — Reefer unit switches off at setpoint, restarts when needed.

**Deck height** — Height of a flatbed or step-deck deck above the ground.

**Drop (tarp)** — How far a tarp hangs down the side: 4′, 6′, 8′.

**Drop-and-hook** — Leaving one trailer and taking another instead of waiting to load.

**Dunnage** — Material used to fill gaps or support cargo (wood, airbags).

**E-track** — Metal rails on van walls for straps and load bars.

**Fifth wheel** — Coupling plate on the tractor that holds the trailer’s kingpin.

**Floor-loaded** — Freight loaded loose on the floor, not on pallets.

**GVWR** — Gross vehicle weight rating: maximum loaded weight of a vehicle.

**Kingpin** — Pin under the trailer’s nose that locks into the fifth wheel.

**Landing gear** — Legs that support a dropped trailer.

**Liftgate** — Powered platform that lowers freight to the ground.

**Load bar** — Bar wedged across a van to hold freight in place.

**Over-dimension** — Freight exceeding legal width, height, length or weight.

**Pallet jack** — Hand tool for moving pallets.

**Payload** — Cargo weight a truck can legally carry.

**Power only** — Tractor pulling a trailer it doesn’t own (PO).

**Pre-cool** — Cooling a reefer trailer before loading, when required.

**Reefer** — Refrigerated, temperature-controlled trailer or truck.

**Setpoint** — The temperature the reefer unit is set to hold.

**Step deck** — Flatbed with an upper and a lower deck (SD).

**Straight truck** — Truck with cab and cargo body on one frame.

**Tandem** — Pair of axles. Trailer tandems often slide.

**Tare** — Empty weight of a vehicle or trailer.

**Tiedown** — Strap, chain or similar used to secure cargo.

**Weigh out** — Reaching the weight limit before the space is full.

**WLL** — Working load limit of a tiedown.
