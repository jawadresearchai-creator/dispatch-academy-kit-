# One message defines the whole job

It is 9:30 PM in Lahore. A text arrives from a small trucking company in Illinois:

WhatsApp · Mike (owner, 3 trucks) · 12:30 PM Central

Truck 12 empties in **Joliet, IL tomorrow 7:30 AM**. 53′ dry van. Driver Carlos will have his full 11 hours of driving tomorrow. We want to go toward the Southeast, need **$2.40+/mile**. Find me something good.

Your answer — by the end of this course:

Booked: Joliet → Charlotte, NC, 750 mi, $2,050. Broker verified, RC checked. PU Wed 9 AM, DEL Thu 2 PM. Directions + PU# sent to Carlos.

To send that answer you must have done **all of this**, in about an hour:

- **Equipment:** which loads fit a 53′ dry van — and which don’t.
- **Geography & time:** where Joliet and Charlotte are, and what 7:30 AM Central is in Pakistan.
- **Hours of service:** can Carlos legally pick up and deliver on time?
- **Load board:** search smartly and shortlist loads.
- **Economics:** is $2,050 for 750 miles, plus empty miles, good for this truck?
- **Broker checks:** is this broker real, authorized, and good at paying?
- **Negotiation:** ask the right questions, then get a better rate.
- **Paperwork:** read the rate confirmation before the carrier signs.
- **Execution:** dispatch the driver and handle what goes wrong.

## What a carrier-side dispatcher does

*Lesson 0.1 · What the job is*

Lesson 0.1 · What the job is

# What a carrier-side dispatcher does

> **[REAL-WORLD TIP] Working definition**
>
> A truck dispatcher works **for a motor carrier**. The job is to **keep the carrier’s trucks loaded with profitable, legal freight**: find loads, check the broker, negotiate, book, send the driver, follow the load until it is delivered, and close the paperwork so the carrier gets paid.

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/loop.jpg)

The dispatcher’s loop. It starts with an empty truck and ends with the next empty truck — several times a day, for every truck you run.

### The dispatcher’s three customers

**The carrier (owner)** pays you and wants profit. **The driver** is your daily partner: they want good miles, home time and clear information. **The broker** is who you buy freight from. You are polite and professional with brokers, but you do not work for them.

### What “good” looks like

A good dispatcher keeps empty miles low and pay per mile high. They avoid fraud and never book a load the driver can’t legally make. Drivers trust what they say, and the paperwork is clean enough that the carrier is paid on time.

> **[KEY RULE] Carrier-side means carrier-side**
>
> In every conversation, ask: **“Is this good for my carrier and my driver?”** Not: “Will this make the broker happy?” Brokers negotiate for their margin. You negotiate for your truck.

### Eight words you will hear every day

#### Load

One shipment you move for pay.

#### Lane

A route between two areas, e.g. Chicago → Atlanta.

#### Deadhead

Empty miles driven to reach a pickup. Unpaid.

#### RPM

Rate per mile: pay ÷ miles.

#### Broker

Middleman between shipper and carrier.

#### RC

Rate confirmation: the load contract.

#### BOL

Bill of lading: freight receipt, signed at pickup.

#### POD

Proof of delivery: BOL signed by the receiver.

## Why small carriers pay for dispatch

*Lesson 0.1 · Why the job exists*

Lesson 0.1 · Why the job exists

# Why small carriers pay for dispatch

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/waffle.jpg)

### The owner-operator problem

Picture Carlos, who owns one truck. While he drives 10 hours a day, loads for tomorrow are posted and taken by other trucks within minutes. He can’t search a load board or call five brokers while driving, and hours-of-service rules limit how long he can work anyway. Every day his truck sits empty, he still pays for the truck, the insurance and his own living costs.

A dispatcher solves exactly this problem. **The carrier drives; the dispatcher plans, sells and handles the paperwork.**

### Who hires dispatchers

| Employer | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Dispatch company** (often U.S.-registered with an office in Pakistan) | Runs many small carriers for a fee. The most common door for Pakistani dispatchers. |
| **Owner-operator / small fleet** | 1–10 trucks, hires one dispatcher directly. Usually after you have experience. |
| **Mid-size fleet** | In-house dispatch team; sometimes remote assistants for night or overflow work. |

> **[REAL-WORLD TIP] The data**
>
> Trucks moved **11.27 billion tons** of U.S. freight in 2024, and the industry earned **$906 billion** in revenue with **3.58 million** professional drivers. (ATA American Trucking Trends 2025.) Most of that capacity sits with small companies — your future clients.

**Owner-operator, 53′ dry van** — the most common client

**Box-truck carrier** — local and regional, lots of small loads

**Power only** — tractor pulls someone else’s trailer

## Six things this job is not

*Lesson 0.1 · What the job is not*

Lesson 0.1 · What the job is not

# Six things this job is not

Most bad advice about dispatching comes from mixing it up with one of these.

#### Not a broker

Brokers sell freight to shippers and buy trucks. You work for the truck. Crossing this line without broker authority is illegal — Lesson 1.3.

#### Not a driver

You never touch the freight. But you must understand the driver’s day, hours and equipment better than most drivers expect.

#### Not a sales job to shippers

You don’t contact factories for freight. Carrier-side dispatchers get loads from brokers and load boards.

#### Not a call-center script

Every call needs decisions: is this load feasible, profitable and safe? A script alone gets you scammed.

#### Not “get rich quick”

Ads that promise $5,000/month from home in 30 days sell courses. Real pay grows with experience and results.

#### Not borrowing logins

Using someone else’s load-board password is against the rules and a fraud red flag. Access comes from the carrier or employer.

## Myth vs reality

| What you may hear | What is actually true |
| --- | --- |
| “You need a U.S. LLC, a website and a DAT subscription before you start.” | Not for the starting route. You can work under a carrier or dispatch company that gives you authorized access. Buy tools only when a real business model needs them. |
| “Dispatchers need a license.” | There is no federal “dispatcher license.” But if you act like a broker, you need broker registration. DAT itself says the answer depends on the services you provide. |
| “Just book the highest rate on the board.” | The highest rate can end in a dead market, need too many empty miles, or be fraud bait. Rate is one input of many (Module 7). |
| “Old code sheets and rules from a PDF are enough.” | Platforms and rules change. This course corrects outdated claims, for example about HOS, payload limits, CDL rules and load-board codes. |
| “Freshers get hired easily.” | Most Lahore job ads in 2026 ask for 6–12 months of experience. You need a plan to get in (Lesson 0.2). |

## What you bring, what the carrier brings

*Lesson 0.2 · Your zero-investment setup*

Lesson 0.2 · Your zero-investment setup

# What you bring, what the carrier brings

“Zero investment” doesn’t mean nobody pays for anything. It means **the carrier or employer already pays for the trucking infrastructure**, and you bring skill, time and a working desk.

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/kit.jpg)

The carrier side of the picture uses real documents from your training manuals: an FMCSA authority certificate, a certificate of insurance, an ELD device and a load-board screen.

### Your minimum desk, honestly

- **Laptop** that can run a browser with 10+ tabs, and a second screen if possible.
- **Internet with a backup.** A broker won’t wait while your connection drops. Keep mobile data ready.
- **Power backup (UPS)** for load-shedding. Budget for this one first.
- **Headset with a good mic** and a quiet corner. Clear sound matters more than accent.
- **Phone with WhatsApp**, because many U.S. drivers text.

### Why the carrier’s things matter to you

**Authority (USDOT/MC)** is what makes a carrier legal to haul for hire. Brokers check it before they book. **Insurance** must be active, or no broker will give the load. The **ELD** records the driver’s hours, which decides what loads are legal to take. The **load-board seat** is where you find freight.

Registration is changing: FMCSA’s new Motus system (2025–26) changes how USDOT and MC (“docket”) numbers are issued and displayed. MC numbers are still on documents and load boards. Module 5 covers this in detail.

## How you get on a load board without paying

*Lesson 0.2 · Getting access the right way*

Lesson 0.2 · Getting access the right way

# How you get on a load board without paying

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/access.jpg)

### The carrier-seat model

DAT, the largest U.S. load board, says it plainly: **“carriers with authority (MC/DOT) can add a seat to their account for their dispatcher partner.”** In practice, your employer or carrier gives you a login under their account. You search and book **for them**, in their name.

Setting up as an independent third-party dispatcher with your own account comes later, and it has more requirements and costs. That is a business decision for after you have experience (Module 11).

### Account security from day one

- Use **your own** username, never a shared one.
- Turn on **MFA** (two-step login). Truckstop now makes MFA part of carrier onboarding.
- Never share one-time codes. A caller asking for your “verification code” is a scammer.
- Log out on shared computers. Don’t save passwords in a browser at an office PC.
- A stolen dispatcher login is how fraudsters book loads in a real carrier’s name.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Why this is serious**
>
> FMCSA publishes warnings about broker and carrier **identity theft**: criminals use stolen accounts, look-alike emails and fake phone numbers to steal freight. Protecting your login protects your carrier’s identity. Module 8 goes deep on fraud.

# Eight things not to buy yet

Every one of these is sold hard to beginners online. For the starting route, all of them are either unnecessary or someone else’s cost.

#### Load-board subscription

**Why not now:** The carrier or employer gives you a seat.

**Maybe later if:** You run your own dispatch business with signed carriers

#### U.S. LLC / company

**Why not now:** You work under an existing company first.

**Maybe later if:** You have carriers and understand the legal setup

#### Website & branding

**Why not now:** Nobody books loads because of your logo.

**Maybe later if:** You market to carriers at scale

#### Paid dialer / VoIP

**Why not now:** Your employer provides phones and numbers.

**Maybe later if:** You need U.S. numbers for your own business

#### Expensive “job-guarantee” course

**Why not now:** This course + practice + a portfolio does the job.

**Maybe later if:** Never — be very wary of guarantees

#### Broker authority

**Why not now:** That is a different business with bonds and liability.

**Maybe later if:** Only if you deliberately choose brokerage

#### CRM / lead lists

**Why not now:** You aren’t selling to carriers yet.

**Maybe later if:** You start finding your own carrier clients

#### Paid ads

**Why not now:** Nothing to advertise at the learning stage.

**Maybe later if:** After you have a proven service

> **[KEY RULE] The rule**
>
> **Buy a tool only when a real job, carrier or client needs it**, and preferably when someone else is paying. Your first investments are skill, a UPS, a headset and time.

## You work while Pakistan sleeps

*Lesson 0.2 · Your working hours*

Lesson 0.2 · Your working hours

# You work while Pakistan sleeps

U.S. shippers, brokers and drivers work U.S. daytime. That is Pakistan evening and night. Pakistan (UTC+5) never changes its clocks, but the U.S. does twice a year, so your shift moves by one hour.

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/time_summer.jpg)

Right now (September 2026) the U.S. is on daylight saving time. Eastern offices open at 5 PM in Pakistan.

### The four zones you need

| Zone | Summer gap Mar–Nov | Winter gap Nov–Mar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Eastern** (ET) | PKT = ET + 9 h | PKT = ET + 10 h |
| **Central** (CT) | PKT = CT + 10 h | PKT = CT + 11 h |
| **Mountain** (MT) | PKT = MT + 11 h | PKT = MT + 12 h |
| **Pacific** (PT) | PKT = PT + 12 h | PKT = PT + 13 h |

Most of Arizona does not use daylight saving time, so it stays at PKT = AZ + 12 h all year. Module 3 covers the exceptions.

### 2026–27 dates to remember

- **Sun 1 Nov 2026:** U.S. clocks go back. Your shift starts an hour later.
- **Sun 14 Mar 2027:** U.S. clocks go forward. Your shift starts an hour earlier.

> **[TRY IT] Try it now**
>
> A broker says: “Pickup is 6 AM Central on Tuesday.” Today is 30 September. What time is that in Pakistan, and on which day? (Answer at the back.)

## After 1 November: the winter shift

*Lesson 0.2 · Your working hours*

Lesson 0.2 · Your working hours

# After 1 November: the winter shift

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/time_winter.jpg)

From 1 Nov 2026 to 14 Mar 2027 every U.S. zone is one more hour behind Pakistan. Eastern offices open at 6 PM PKT.

### What the night really looks like

Shippers and receivers often start **earlier than offices**. Docks can open at 5–6 AM local time, and drivers are on the road even earlier. West Coast brokers are still working at 4–5 AM in Pakistan. Many Pakistani dispatch offices therefore run a **5 PM–2 AM or 6 PM–3 AM** shift and have someone watch late problems.

### Make the night sustainable

- Keep one fixed sleep block, for example 5 AM–12 PM, blackout curtains included.
- Eat a proper meal before your shift, not at 3 AM.
- Keep a U.S. clock strip on your screen (ET/CT/MT/PT).
- Protect Friday night: it is U.S. Friday, one of the busiest days.

### Quick converter (winter)

| U.S. local | ET → PKT | CT → PKT | PT → PKT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 AM | 4 PM | 5 PM | 7 PM |
| 8 AM | 6 PM | 7 PM | 9 PM |
| 10 AM | 8 PM | 9 PM | 11 PM |
| 12 PM | 10 PM | 11 PM | 1 AM\* |
| 3 PM | 1 AM\* | 2 AM\* | 4 AM\* |
| 5 PM | 3 AM\* | 4 AM\* | 6 AM\* |

\* = next calendar day in Pakistan. A load that picks up “Monday 3 PM ET” is **Tuesday 1 AM** for you. Always write both dates.

> **[KEY RULE] Write every time with its zone**
>
> Never write “pickup 8:00.” Write **“PU Tue 8:00 AM CT (Tue 7 PM PKT)”**. Most expensive dispatch mistakes start with a missing time zone.

# Three realistic doors into your first job

#### Door 1 · Entry roles

Ads where experience is “preferred,” not required. Also **dispatch assistant**, **driver onboarding** or **carrier sales** roles at dispatch companies. You learn the live systems there, then move to a dispatch seat.

#### Door 2 · Prove it in the interview

Many companies test you with a **mock broker call** and questions about load boards and documents. If you can run a clean call and read a rate confirmation, you stand out against people who only watched videos.

#### Door 3 · Direct carrier (later)

After 6–12 months you can dispatch directly for owner-operators, as a contractor paid by the carrier. That is where your own business model begins (Module 11).

## One load, six players

Lesson 1.1 · The players

# One load, six players

Every load you ever book has the same cast. The photos and document snippets below come from one real load that we follow through this whole lesson: **load #12354, Lebanon, Ohio → Owensboro, Kentucky.**

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/actors.jpg)

### Read the picture left to right

The **shipper** has freight to move. It hires a **broker**, who posts the load and looks for a truck. The broker books a **carrier**, and the carrier’s **dispatcher** (you) negotiates and books that load. The carrier’s **driver** picks it up and delivers it to the **receiver**, who signs for it.

### Where you sit

You sit **inside the carrier’s business**, not between the broker and the carrier. That one idea decides who pays you, whose interests you defend on a call, and which activities are legal for you (Lesson 1.3).

> **[KEY RULE] Memorise it as a chain**
>
> **Shipper → Broker → Carrier (Dispatcher + Driver) → Receiver.** When someone on the phone confuses you, ask yourself: which link in the chain is this person?

## Shipper, broker, carrier

*Lesson 1.1 · The players up close*

Lesson 1.1 · The players up close

# Shipper, broker, carrier

SHIPPER

The company whose goods need to move: a factory, a warehouse or a distributor. On load #12354 the shipper is **Green Bay Packaging** in Lebanon, Ohio, which makes printed cardboard boxes. The shipper creates the **bill of lading** and loads the trailer.

#### What they want

Freight picked up on time, loaded safely, delivered on time and undamaged, at a good price.

#### How you deal with them

Usually **not directly**. The broker is your contact. Your driver meets shipping staff at the dock. You may call the shipper only for practical questions, if the broker allows it.

**Words:** consignor · shipping hours · appointment vs FCFS (first come, first served) · dock · live load vs drop trailer

BROKER

A licensed middleman. It has contracts with shippers and finds carriers to move their loads, earning the difference between what the shipper pays and what it pays the carrier. On our load: **Ship Express LLC**, MC 347749. The broker posts loads on load boards and issues the **rate confirmation**.

#### What they want

A reliable truck at the lowest workable rate, fast updates, tracking turned on, no surprises for their shipper.

#### How you deal with them

Your main phone contact. Verify them, ask questions, negotiate, follow their rules, keep them updated. Professional, never desperate.

**Words:** 3PL · freight broker · MC number · rate con · tracking (e.g. MacroPoint) · broker credit / days-to-pay

CARRIER

The company that owns the truck and has **operating authority** to haul for hire (USDOT and, today, an MC number). It can be one owner-operator or a fleet. On our load the carrier is a small company running a **26′ straight box truck**.

#### What they want

Loads that pay well, few empty miles, trucks moving every day, paid quickly, drivers who stay.

#### How you deal with them

**Your client or employer.** You follow their preferences: lanes, minimum rates, home time, types of freight they refuse.

**Words:** owner-operator · fleet · authority · MC/DOT · COI (insurance certificate) · floor rate

## Driver, receiver — and the supporting cast

*Lesson 1.1 · The players up close*

Lesson 1.1 · The players up close

# Driver, receiver — and the supporting cast

DRIVER

The professional behind the wheel, either an employee or the owner-operator himself. The driver inspects the truck, checks the freight count at pickup, signs the BOL, drives within hours-of-service limits and gets the POD signed at delivery.

#### What they want

Good paying miles, predictable schedules, home time, clear and complete instructions, and respect.

#### How you deal with them

Daily, by phone and WhatsApp. You plan **with** drivers, not **at** them. A driver who trusts you will tell you about problems early.

**Words:** HOS · ELD · check call · ETA · PU / DEL · detention · layover

RECEIVER

Also called the **consignee**: the place the freight goes. On our load, **Tracco** in Owensboro, Kentucky. It unloads, counts, notes any damage and signs the BOL, which turns it into the **proof of delivery**.

#### What they want

A truck that arrives on its appointment with the correct count and no damage.

#### How you deal with them

Mostly through the driver and broker. Appointment changes normally go through the broker.

**Words:** consignee · receiving hours · lumper (paid unloader) · OS&D (over, short and damaged)

## The supporting cast

#### Factoring company

Pays the carrier quickly for its invoices, then collects from the broker, for a fee. A **Notice of Assignment (NOA)** tells brokers to pay the factor.

#### Tracking app

Apps such as MacroPoint share the truck’s location with the broker. Our RC made tracking mandatory, with a $150 penalty.

#### Load board

The marketplace where brokers post loads and carriers search, e.g. DAT and Truckstop. Module 6.

#### FMCSA

The U.S. federal regulator. It grants authority and publishes company records used to verify carriers and brokers.

## Freight, paper and money move differently

*Lesson 1.1 · Three flows*

Lesson 1.1 · Three flows

# Freight, paper and money move differently

Beginners picture one line from shipper to receiver. Experienced dispatchers see **three lines at the same time**, and know that problems happen where they don’t match.

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/flows.jpg)

#### Freight

Moves in hours or days. Your job: the right truck, at the right place, at the right time, and a legal driver.

#### Paper

Every step leaves a document. If it isn’t on paper, it didn’t happen: no RC, no agreement; no POD, no payment.

#### Money

Moves last and in the **opposite direction**. Brokers often pay 15–45 days after receiving the paperwork, which is why carriers use factoring.

> **[KEY RULE] Your job touches all three**
>
> You **arrange** the freight, you **check and collect** the paper, and you **protect** the money by booking with brokers who pay and by sending clean paperwork fast. But the broker’s money never passes through your hands (Lesson 1.3).

## The rate confirmation

# The rate confirmation

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/rc_annot.jpg)

### Why it matters

The RC is the **legal agreement for this one load** between the broker and the carrier. Whatever was said on the phone, **the RC is what counts**. If a detention or layover rate isn’t written in it, expect a fight later.

This RC is also a good example of **penalty clauses**: $150 for no tracking, $150 for a late POD, and no TONU (truck order not used) payment if the load is cancelled. None of them is unusual. All of them must be **explained to the driver**.

>
> Before any signature: **1** carrier name and MC correct · **2** rate matches what you agreed · **3** pickup and delivery addresses, dates and times (with zones) · **4** equipment and weight · **5** special rules and penalties · **6** accessorial terms (detention, layover, TONU).

# The bill of lading (BOL)

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/bol_annot.jpg)

### Why it matters

The BOL is created by the **shipper**. It says what was loaded, how many pieces and how much weight, and where it goes. When the driver signs it at pickup, the carrier accepts the freight “in apparent good order.” The BOL is also a **contract of carriage**: the fine print explains the terms.

>

## The proof of delivery (POD)

*Lesson 1.1 · Document 3 of 3*

Lesson 1.1 · Document 3 of 3

# The proof of delivery (POD)

![](/assets/diagrams/m00-01/pod_annot.jpg)

### Why it matters

The POD is simply the **same BOL, signed by the receiver**, often with a stamp, date, piece count and a space for exceptions. It proves the carrier finished the job. **Brokers pay against the POD**, so it is the most valuable paper in trucking.

On this load the receiver wrote **Qty Rcvd 10** and left Over / Short / Damaged blank. That is a clean delivery, and the carrier can invoice.

> **[KEY RULE] POD habits**
>
> Get a **clear, flat, readable photo** of every page right after delivery, before the driver leaves the dock. Check that it is signed, dated and shows the count. Send it the same day with the invoice.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] If anything is written in OS&D**
>
> Call the broker **immediately**, tell the carrier, and get photos of the freight. A note like “2 pallets damaged” can become a claim deducted from the rate.

# Who pays whom on load #12354

### The payment chain

- The **shipper pays the broker** an amount the carrier never sees.
- The **broker pays the carrier** the RC rate, here $700, after receiving the invoice, RC and POD. That is often weeks later.

## The legal line

*Lesson 1.3 · Dispatcher vs broker*

Lesson 1.3 · Dispatcher vs broker

# The legal line

This is the most important boundary in your career. Stay on the carrier side of it and you are doing a legitimate job. Cross it without broker registration and you are brokering illegally, even if your title says “dispatcher.”

#### Broker — federal definition (summary)

A person who, **for compensation, arranges or offers to arrange** transportation of property by an authorized motor carrier. A carrier’s own employees and **bona fide agents** are not brokers when they arrange loads for that carrier. (49 CFR 371.2(a))

#### Bona fide agent — federal definition

People “who are part of the normal organization of a motor carrier and perform duties under the carrier’s directions pursuant to a **preexisting agreement which provides for a continuing relationship**, precluding the exercise of discretion on the part of the agent in **allocating traffic** between the carrier and others.” (49 CFR 371.2(b))

## What FMCSA’s 2023 guidance says about dispatch services

Generally NOT a broker when the dispatch service…

- has a **written contract** with the motor carrier appointing it as agent
- **does not seek or solicit shippers** for freight
- is **paid only by the motor carrier**
- is **not an intermediary** in the broker–carrier payment
- does **not re-assign** the load to another carrier

Needs broker registration when it…

- negotiates freight **directly with a shipper**
- takes **compensation from a broker or factoring company** for a load
- **accepts a shipment without a truck**, then looks for one
- **allocates traffic**: accepts a load that could go to more than one carrier and chooses who gets it

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] What is at stake**
>
> Unauthorized brokerage can bring civil penalties of up to **$10,000 per violation**, plus liability to injured parties for all valid claims. Officers and principals can be personally liable (49 U.S.C. 14916). Brokers must register with FMCSA and file **$75,000** of financial security (49 CFR 387.307).

This course teaches compliance awareness, not legal advice. For your own business structure later, speak to a U.S. transportation attorney.

Call the broker immediately, inform the carrier, and get photos of the freight and the signed POD. Document everything for a possible claim.

**1.10.**

Any four: negotiating directly with shippers; taking pay from a broker or factor; accepting a load before having a truck; allocating a load between carriers; handling broker-to-carrier payments.

**1.11.**

The **carrier only**, or your employer, which is paid by its carriers.

**1.12.**

A check call is you getting status **from the driver** (location, loaded, count, ETA). A broker update is you **telling the broker** that status, proactively.

| Case | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | GREEN | Written agreements; the load is booked for one specific carrier with a truck. Normal dispatch. |
| **B** | RED | Negotiating directly with a shipper and accepting freight without a truck (Q3, Q4). |
| **C** | RED | Pay from a broker (Q2). It is also a conflict of interest against your carrier. |
| **D** | RED | Accepting a load and then choosing between carriers is allocating traffic (Q4, Q5). |
| **E** | GREEN | Paid by the carrier, and broker money goes to the carrier or its factor directly. |
| **F** | RED | You are in the payment chain (Q6), and it is a major fraud risk. |
| **G** | GREEN | A normal operational problem, not a legal one. Handle it professionally (Module 10). |
| **H** | AMBER | No written agreement (Q1). Get a signed dispatch agreement before working. |

# Glossary

**Accessorial** — Extra charge on top of the line-haul rate: detention, layover, lumper, TONU.

**Appointment** — A fixed pickup or delivery time slot. The opposite is FCFS.

**Bill of lading (BOL)** — Shipper’s document listing the freight; receipt and carriage contract, signed at pickup.

**Bona fide agent** — Someone who works within a carrier’s organization under a continuing agreement. A carrier-side dispatcher aims to be this.

**Broker** — Licensed middleman who arranges transport between shippers and carriers for compensation.

**Carrier** — Company with authority that owns or operates trucks and hauls freight for hire.

**Check call** — A status call or text with the driver: location, loaded, ETA.

**COI** — Certificate of insurance: proof the carrier’s insurance is active.

**Consignee** — The receiver of the freight.

**Deadhead** — Empty miles driven without paid freight, e.g. to reach a pickup.

**Detention** — Payment for waiting too long at a shipper or receiver, if the RC allows it.

**ELD** — Electronic logging device: records the driver’s hours of service.

**ETA** — Estimated time of arrival. Always give it with a time zone.

**Factoring** — Selling invoices to a factoring company for fast cash, minus a fee.

**FCFS** — First come, first served: no appointment, but open hours.

**FMCSA** — Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, the U.S. trucking regulator.

**HOS** — Hours of service: federal limits on driving and duty time.

**Lane** — An origin–destination route, e.g. Chicago → Atlanta.

**Load board** — Online marketplace of posted loads, e.g. DAT, Truckstop.

**Lumper** — A third party paid to unload freight at a receiver.

**MC number** — FMCSA operating-authority docket number, still widely used in 2026.

**NOA** — Notice of assignment: tells brokers to pay a carrier’s factoring company.

**Owner-operator** — A driver who owns his truck and often his own authority.

**POD** — Proof of delivery: the BOL signed by the receiver.

**Rate confirmation (RC)** — The broker–carrier agreement for one load: rate, stops, terms.

**RPM** — Rate per mile = pay ÷ miles.

**Shipper** — The company sending the freight.

**TONU** — Truck order not used: payment when a booked load is cancelled after the truck is dispatched, if the RC allows it.

**USDOT number** — FMCSA identifier for a company operating commercial vehicles; its safety record is tied to it.
