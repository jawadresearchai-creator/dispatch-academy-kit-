# How to use this module

You don’t need to become a geography teacher. You need **speed**: when a load pops up, you should know in five seconds roughly where it goes, which way the truck will travel, which interstates it will use, and what the appointment is in Pakistan time.

- **Day 1 (1.5 h):** states and codes. Do the state drill until you score 24/24.

## A load posting is a tiny map

*Lesson 3.1 · Why geography*

Lesson 3.1 · Why geography

# A load posting is a tiny map

A typical load-board line

**09/30**   V   **JOLIET, IL**   DH-O 12   →   **CHARLOTTE, NC**   42,000 lb   53′   Full

### What an experienced dispatcher “sees” in that line

- **Where:** Joliet is just south-west of Chicago. Charlotte is in North Carolina, in the Southeast.
- **Which way:** south-east, around 750–800 miles. A full day and a half of driving.
- **Which roads:** down through Indianapolis, Cincinnati and West Virginia (or a similar route).
- **When:** Joliet is Central Time; Charlotte is Eastern. The clock moves forward one hour.
- **After:** Charlotte is a decent freight market — the truck should find a reload.

### What a beginner sees

Two names and a number. Then they book it, and find out afterwards that the delivery is in the wrong time zone, the driver can’t make it, or the truck is stuck in a place with no freight going home.

> **[KEY RULE] Geography comes before rates**
>
> You can’t judge a rate, check hours of service, or plan a reload until you know where the load is going. That is why this module comes before HOS (Module 4) and load economics (Module 7).

## The 50 states and their codes

*Lesson 3.1 · States and codes*

Lesson 3.1 · States and codes

# The 50 states and their codes

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/map_states.jpg)

### How codes appear on real paperwork

Always **City, ST**: “Lebanon, OH”, “Owensboro, KY”. The two letters are U.S. Postal Service codes, the same everywhere: load boards, rate confirmations, BOLs, ELDs.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] The same city name in many states**
>
> There are Springfields in IL, MO, MA, OH and more; Columbus in OH, GA and MS; Portland in OR and ME. **Never write a city without its state**, and repeat it on the phone: “Portland, **Oregon**?”

### A study method that works

1. Learn the **big shapes first**: CA, TX, FL, the Great Lakes, the East Coast.
2. Then learn **by region** (next pages), not alphabetically.
3. Then drill the **confusing codes** (page 6).
4. Test yourself on the blank-map drill (page 10) until you score 100%.

Alaska and Hawaii appear in the corner (not to scale). You will rarely dispatch there; the lower 48 states are where almost all truckload work happens.

# DAT zones: search a whole region at once

### How dispatchers use zones

DAT One lets you type a zone instead of a city: a destination of **Z3** finds loads going anywhere in AL, FL, GA, MS or TN. You can enter several: **Z7, Z8, Z9** (or Z7Z8Z9).

Zones are perfect for **“where can my truck go next?”** searches, and for a carrier who says “anything heading Southeast.”

## The numbering code

*Lesson 3.2 · Interstates*

Lesson 3.2 · Interstates

# The numbering code

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/numbering.jpg)

### FHWA’s rules, in plain words

- **Odd numbers run north–south.** The lowest (I-5) is on the West Coast; the highest (I-95) on the East Coast.
- **Even numbers run east–west.** The lowest (I-10) is in the far south; the highest (I-90, I-94) in the north.
- **Numbers ending in 0 or 5** are the long, major routes: I-5, I-10, I-35, I-40, I-80, I-95…
- **Three digits:** the last two digits are the parent route. An **even first digit** means a loop or connector (I-285 around Atlanta); an **odd first digit** means a spur (I-575 off I-75).

> **[TRY IT] Use the code**
>
> Without a map: is I-65 more east or west than I-35? Does I-20 run north–south or east–west, and is it north or south of I-40? (Answers at the back.)

> **[FACT] Interstates are the freight backbone**
>
> FHWA’s Primary Highway Freight System is about 41,800 miles, and about 38,000 of those miles are Interstate. Most long-haul truckload miles run on these roads.

## Freight corridors

*Lesson 3.2 · Corridors*

Lesson 3.2 · Corridors

# Freight corridors

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/map_corridors.jpg)

### Why corridors matter to a dispatcher

- **Reloads are easier** along a busy corridor: cities along it generate loads in both directions.
- **Your carrier’s preferred lanes** are often corridors: “We like I-35 and I-40.”
- **Ports and borders** feed corridors: I-5 and I-10 from the California ports, I-35 from Laredo, I-95 from the East Coast ports.

> **[KEY RULE] Plan in corridors, not single loads**
>
> An owner-operator from Dallas may want to run **I-35 north** and come back **I-35 south** each week. Knowing the corridor helps you find the second load before the first one delivers.

Descriptions show long-standing freight flows. Volumes and rates on each corridor change with season and economy; check live data (Module 7).

## Lane 1: Dallas, TX → Atlanta, GA

*Lesson 3.2 · Reading a lane*

Lesson 3.2 · Reading a lane

# Lane 1: Dallas, TX → Atlanta, GA

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/route_dal_atl.jpg)

### The five questions for every lane

1. **Direction:** which way is the truck moving?
2. **Main interstate(s):** which 1–3 roads carry most of the trip?
3. **Distance:** roughly how many miles, or how many driving days?
4. **Time zones:** the zone of each stop, and any change on the way.
5. **One risk:** mountains, weather, a big city, a border, a weak market for the next load.

> **[TRY IT] Worked example: the time-zone trap**
>
> On day 2 the truck leaves **Birmingham, AL at 2:00 PM CT** for the last 150 miles to Atlanta, about 2.5 hours. It arrives around **4:30 PM on Central time**, but the receiver works on Atlanta time: **5:30 PM ET**. If receiving closes at 5:00 PM ET, the truck is late, even though a Central-time plan said he had time.

Real driving time depends on hours-of-service rules, traffic and breaks (Module 4). Here we only practise the clock.

### Several interstates, one direction

Long lanes often use several interstates. You don’t have to memorise every change; know the **big turning points**: Indianapolis, Cincinnati, then the mountains of West Virginia down to Charlotte.

This is the lane from Module 0’s message. At about 750–800 miles it is **more than one legal driving day**, so the pickup and delivery times must allow for a 10-hour break (Module 4).

> I-77 through West Virginia and Virginia has long grades and can see snow and fog in winter. Mountain routes are slower and use more fuel. Ask the driver about conditions, and don’t plan tight appointments through the mountains in winter.

## Lane 3: Laredo, TX → Chicago, IL

*Lesson 3.2 · Reading a lane*

Lesson 3.2 · Reading a lane

# Lane 3: Laredo, TX → Chicago, IL

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/route_lar_chi.jpg)

### Cross-border freight

Laredo sits on the Mexican border and is the busiest U.S.–Mexico land port for freight. Mexican trucks bring loads across. U.S. carriers often pick up at warehouses in **Laredo** and haul north on **I-35** into Texas and on to the Midwest.

The whole lane stays in **Central Time**, a rare easy one. But at about 1,400+ miles it is a **2–3 day** trip for a solo driver.

> **[FACT] Border words you will hear**
>
> **Cross-dock** — freight moved from a Mexican trailer to a U.S. trailer in a warehouse. **Transload** — similar. **Bonded / in-bond** — freight still under customs control; it needs special handling and carrier authorisation. Treat in-bond loads as specialist until trained.

### Major land border crossings for trucks (awareness)

| Crossing | Border | Main interstate from there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Laredo, TX** | Mexico | I-35 north (Texas → Midwest) |
| **El Paso, TX** | Mexico | I-10 east/west, I-25 north |
| **Otay Mesa (San Diego), CA** | Mexico | I-5 / I-805 / I-15 north |
| **Detroit, MI** | Canada (Windsor) | I-75, I-94, I-96 |
| **Buffalo, NY** | Canada (Ontario) | I-90, I-190 |

Cross-border work needs extra documents and carrier set-up (customs brokers, in-bond rules, Canadian/Mexican authority). As a beginner, you mostly dispatch the U.S. side, from a warehouse near the border.

### Back to the real load from Module 1

Lebanon, Ohio is just north-east of Cincinnati. Owensboro, Kentucky is on the Ohio River in western Kentucky. The truck runs down toward **Louisville**, then west, and crosses from **Eastern into Central Time** on the way.

## A truck is not a car: routes, restrictions, seasons

*Lesson 3.2 · Truck routing*

Lesson 3.2 · Truck routing

# A truck is not a car: routes, restrictions, seasons

#### Truck GPS, not car GPS

A car route from Google Maps can send a 13′6″ truck under a 12′ bridge, onto a parkway where trucks are banned, or down a road with a weight limit. Drivers use truck-specific GPS; dispatchers use truck mileage tools.

#### Miles: road vs straight line

Road miles are always more than the straight-line distance on a map, sometimes much more around lakes, mountains and cities. Rates are paid on the miles on the RC or the broker’s mileage system; check them.

## Classic restrictions and seasonal risks

| Situation | What happens | Dispatcher habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New York parkways** | Commercial vehicles are banned from parkways around NYC and Long Island; low bridges catch trucks every year | Deliveries in NYC/NJ: driver must follow truck routes |
| **Big-city delivery** | Tight docks, traffic, time windows, tolls | Ask about access; allow extra time |
| **Mountain passes** | Long grades on I-70 (Colorado), I-80 (Wyoming/Utah), I-5 (Grapevine, Siskiyou), I-77/I-81 (Appalachians) | Allow more time; ask about weather and chains |
| **Winter closures** | Snow, ice and wind can close parts of I-80 in Wyoming, I-70 in Colorado and others | Check road conditions; build buffer days |
| **Hurricanes / floods** | Gulf and Atlantic coasts, summer–autumn | Watch forecasts for FL, TX, LA, the Carolinas |
| **Hazmat routes** | Some tunnels and routes ban hazmat (e.g. Colorado’s Eisenhower Tunnel on I-70) | Hazmat loads need hazmat routing |

## Where will the truck be after this load?

Lesson 3.2 · Thinking ahead

# Where will the truck be after this load?

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/nextload.jpg)

### Headhaul, backhaul and dead ends

**Headhaul** = the direction where freight is plentiful and pays well. **Backhaul** = the return direction, often with fewer loads or lower rates. Some destinations sit at the **end of the map** (the Florida peninsula, the far Northeast, remote Mountain West areas), so trucks often leave with lower rates or empty miles.

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] Don’t hard-code market opinions**
>
> Many dispatchers say “Florida is mostly inbound” or “the Northeast is hard to get out of.” Such patterns shift with season (produce, holidays) and the economy. Treat them as **questions to check with live load-to-truck data** (Module 7), not facts.

## Convert any appointment

*Lesson 3.3 · Conversion*

Lesson 3.3 · Conversion

# Convert any appointment

![](/assets/diagrams/m03/convert.jpg)

### The gap table (memorise)

| Zone | Mar – Nov | Nov – Mar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ET** | +9 h | +10 h |
| **CT** | +10 h | +11 h |
| **MT** | +11 h | +12 h |
| **Arizona** | +12 h | +12 h |
| **PT** | +12 h | +13 h |

> **[KEY RULE] Going the other way**
>
> A driver calls at **11:30 PM PKT** in October and says “I’ll be there in 3 hours.” Where is he? In Central Time it is 1:30 PM, so he arrives around 4:30 PM CT. Subtract the gap to get U.S. time; add it to get Pakistan time.

> **[FACT] Tool, not crutch**
>
> Keep a world clock on your screen with ET, CT, MT, PT and PKT. But learn the gaps by heart: on a fast broker call you won’t have time to search.

## Seven time traps — and how to avoid them

*Lesson 3.3 · Time traps*

Lesson 3.3 · Time traps

# Seven time traps — and how to avoid them

| # | Trap | Example | Habit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Different zones at each end** | Dallas pickup CT, Atlanta delivery ET | Write each stop in its own zone |
| 2 | **Zone change mid-trip** | Lebanon OH (ET) → Owensboro KY (CT): the clock goes back | Calculate the ETA in the receiver’s zone |
| 3 | **Date change in Pakistan** | “Mon 4 PM ET” in winter = **Tue** 2 AM PKT | Write the day with every PKT time |
| 4 | **Clock-change weekends** | Sun 1 Nov 2026 and Sun 14 Mar 2027 — your gaps change overnight | Put both dates in your calendar |
| 5 | **Arizona** | Phoenix = LA time in summer, Denver time in winter | Treat AZ as “+12 all year” |
| 6 | **Split states** | El Paso TX is Mountain; Pensacola FL is Central | Check the city, not the state |
| 7 | **The driver’s log clock** | Federal rules require the driver’s record of duty status to use the time standard of the driver’s **home terminal**, which may differ from the local appointment time | When discussing hours, confirm which clock the driver means |

> **[WARNING / CORRECTION] A real-world cost**
>
> An appointment missed by one hour can mean a **rescheduled delivery the next day**, a lost layover day for the driver, a late fee on the RC, or the broker giving the next load to someone else. Most of these mistakes are pure clock errors.

> **[SAY IT OUT LOUD] Say it out loud: confirming an appointment**
>
> “So delivery is **Thursday, 8 AM Eastern**, at the receiver in Charlotte — correct?” Always say the **day**, the **time** and the **zone**.

> **[KEY RULE] In your load notes**
>
> PU **Wed 09:00 CT** (Wed 19:00 PKT) · DEL **Thu 08:00 ET** (Thu 17:00 PKT). 24-hour format avoids AM/PM mistakes.

## Four worked scenarios

*Lesson 3.3 · Worked scenarios*

Lesson 3.3 · Worked scenarios

# Four worked scenarios

#### A · Summer, Chicago shipper

Broker: “Pickup is tomorrow, Wednesday, 6:00 AM at the shipper in Chicago.” It is 30 September.  
  
**Chicago = CT, summer → +10.** 6:00 AM CT = **4:00 PM Wednesday PKT**. The driver should be rolling there during your early shift. Confirm by 3 PM PKT.

#### B · Winter, New Jersey receiver

Delivery Friday 2:00 PM in Newark, NJ, in December.  
  
**Newark = ET, winter → +10.** 2:00 PM ET = **12:00 AM (midnight) Saturday PKT**. The POD will arrive after midnight your time: note it as Saturday.

#### C · Phoenix, July

Receiver in Phoenix opens at 5:00 AM.  
  
**Arizona = MST all year → +12.** 5:00 AM = **5:00 PM PKT**. In July that is also 5:00 AM in Los Angeles, since AZ has the same clock as PT in summer.

#### D · Cross-zone ETA

Driver leaves Nashville (CT) at 1:00 PM CT for Knoxville (ET), about 3 hours away.  
  
Arrives about 4:00 PM **CT** = **5:00 PM ET** Knoxville time. If the receiver closes at 4:30 PM ET, he will be late. Same state, different zones!

> **[KEY RULE] The pattern in every scenario**
>
> **Find the zone → apply the season gap → check the date → think about the receiver’s clock.** Four steps, every time, until they become automatic.

## Glossary

*Reference*

Reference

# Glossary

**Backhaul** — The return direction of a lane, often with fewer or cheaper loads.

**Beltway / loop** — Interstate that circles a city (even-first-digit 3-digit number).

**Border crossing / port of entry** — Official crossing point with Mexico or Canada.

**Census region** — Official U.S. regions: Northeast, Midwest, South, West.

**Corridor** — A route with large, steady freight volumes.

**CT / ET / MT / PT** — Central, Eastern, Mountain, Pacific time.

**DAT zone** — Load-board region code Z0–Z9 used for searches.

**Daylight saving time (DST)** — U.S. summer time, second Sunday in March to first Sunday in November.

**DH-O / DH-D** — Deadhead to origin / from destination on a load board.

**Headhaul** — The direction where freight is plentiful and pays well.

**Home terminal time** — The time standard the driver’s log must use.

**Hub** — City or area that generates freight all year.

**Hot market** — An area where loads outnumber trucks right now.

**I-xx** — Interstate highway number.

**In-bond** — Freight still under customs control; specialist handling.

**Lane** — Origin → destination pair, e.g. Dallas → Atlanta.

**MST** — Mountain Standard Time; Arizona uses it all year.

**PHFS** — Primary Highway Freight System (FHWA).

**PKT** — Pakistan Standard Time, UTC+5, no DST.

**Road miles** — Actual driving miles, always more than straight-line distance.

**Split state** — A state with two time zones.

**Spur** — Short interstate branching off a main route (odd first digit).

**Texas triangle** — Dallas–Houston–San Antonio/Austin freight region.

**Truck route** — Road legal and safe for large trucks.

> **[FACT] How the maps were made**
>
> State shapes come from U.S. Census boundary data. Interstates are drawn as straight lines between the major cities on each route, so they are simplified. Time-zone areas were built county by county; a few boundary counties in split states are approximate. Distances are rounded “about” figures; use a truck mileage tool for real trips.
